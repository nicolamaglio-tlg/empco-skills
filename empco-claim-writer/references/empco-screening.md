# EmpCo screening reference

Operational screening only. Directive (EU) 2024/825 ("EmpCo") amends the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD) and the Consumer Rights Directive; member states apply it from 27 September 2026 through national law. The summaries below are paraphrases — verify against the Official Journal text and current national law before relying on them.

## Key definitions

- **Environmental claim:** any message or representation, in any form (text, image, graphic, symbol, label, brand or company name, product name), that states or implies a product, category, brand, or trader has a positive or zero environmental impact, less impact than others, or has improved its impact over time.
- **Generic environmental claim:** an environmental claim whose specification isn't given in clear and prominent terms on the same medium. Typical examples: "environmentally friendly", "eco-friendly", "green", "nature's friend", "ecological", "climate friendly", "gentle on the environment", "carbon friendly", "biodegradable" (unqualified).
  - *Specification has to be close.* Treat a generic claim as specified only if the specification sits next to it or is explicitly linked from it (a footnote marker, "see how", a link on the claim itself). Figures elsewhere on the page, under other headings, don't cure it.
  - *Generic claim vs. vague process language.* The prohibited pattern (point 4a below) is about claims of environmental excellence for a product, brand, or business — "eco-friendly", "green", "sustainable", "climate friendly". Descriptions of what a business does or is working on — "we manage chemicals carefully", "we work to reduce our water footprint" — are not generic claims in that sense. If they're vague, treat them as context-dependent (substantiation, scope), usually medium priority. A textbook generic term should never rank below vague process language on the same page.
- **Recognised excellent environmental performance:** performance in line with the EU Ecolabel, an officially recognised EN ISO 14024 type I ecolabel scheme in the member state, or top environmental performance under applicable Union law.
- **Sustainability label:** a voluntary trust mark, quality mark, or equivalent, public or private, that sets apart or promotes a product, process, or business by reference to its environmental or social characteristics. Mandatory labels are excluded.
- **Certification scheme:** third-party verification with requirements that are publicly available, developed with relevant stakeholders, non-discriminatory, and monitored independently of the scheme owner and the trader.

## Potential prohibited patterns (always unfair — UCPD Annex I, as amended)

Flag these as high priority for review:

1. **Unauthorised sustainability labels** — a sustainability label not based on a certification scheme and not established by public authorities (point 2a). Includes self-created badges and marks.
2. **Unsubstantiated generic claims** — a generic environmental claim where the trader can't show recognised excellent environmental performance relevant to the claim (point 4a).
3. **Whole-product or whole-business claims from one aspect** — an environmental claim about the entire product or business when it's true only of a part or aspect (point 4b). "Made with recycled materials" on packaging-only recycled content; "sustainable brand" from one product line.
4. **Offset-based product GHG claims** — claiming a product has a neutral, reduced, or positive greenhouse-gas impact on the basis of offsetting emissions outside its value chain (point 4c). "Climate neutral", "CO₂-compensated", "carbon positive" at product level.
5. **Legal requirements as a distinctive benefit** — presenting something every product in the category must do by law as a feature of this offer (point 10a). "Free of [substance already banned]".
6. **Durability, repair, and replacement practices** (points 23d–23j) — false claims about durability in usage time or intensity; presenting a good as repairable when it isn't; withholding that a software update will impair function, or presenting an update as necessary when it only adds features; marketing a good with a feature introduced to limit its durability; pushing consumers to replace consumables earlier than necessary; withholding that using third-party consumables or spare parts will impair function.

## Context-dependent issues (misleading depending on the full context — UCPD Articles 6 and 7, as amended)

- **Future commitments** — claims about future environmental performance ("net zero by 2040") without clear, objective, publicly available, verifiable commitments in a detailed and realistic implementation plan with measurable, time-bound targets, periodically verified by an independent third-party expert whose findings are public.
- **Irrelevant benefits** — advertising a benefit that doesn't derive from any feature of the product or business.
- **Comparisons** — comparing environmental or social characteristics without information on the method, the products or traders compared, and how the information is kept up to date.
- **Environmental and social characteristics** — misleading presentation of the product's nature, composition, durability, repairability, recyclability, or social aspects (working conditions, fair pay, animal welfare, charitable contributions).
- **Names and presentation** — brand, product, or company names, colour schemes, and imagery that create an overall green impression the evidence doesn't support.
- **Evidence-scope mismatch** — the claim is broader than what's measured.
- **Missing prominent qualifications** — the qualification exists but doesn't travel with the claim.
- **Causation and product linkage** — outcomes attributed to the product or the business without evidence of the causal link.

## Mandatory information

Information the law requires — packaging disposal and sorting instructions, recycling codes, energy labels, material composition labels — is not a claim in itself. Note it and rule it out. It becomes a potential prohibited pattern only when it's presented as a distinctive benefit of this offer (point 10a above), for example "our packaging comes with recycling instructions" as a selling point.

## Insufficient information

Use this bucket when the claim might be fine but the reviewed material doesn't show enough to tell — the substantiation, scheme, or scope sits somewhere you haven't seen.

## Evidence principles

- The claim cannot be broader than the evidence.
- Scope needed to prevent a misleading impression must travel with the claim.
- A component result does not establish a whole-product benefit.
- Measurement does not automatically establish causation.
- Landscape evidence must be linked to the supplier, ingredient, programme, batch, or SKU being marketed.
- Measurement, assurance, certification, and legal review are separate.

Never conclude from an automated screen that a claim is lawful or unlawful.
