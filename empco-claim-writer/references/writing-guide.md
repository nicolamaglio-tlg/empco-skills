# Writing claim directions

A direction only lowers risk if the evidence behind it exists. Every direction has to say what evidence it needs; a direction the business can't substantiate is not a safer option, just a different risk.

## Shapes of direction

Order them from most conservative to most ambitious. Common shapes:

- **Specific and scoped** — name the attribute, the part of the product it applies to, the metric, and the baseline. "Made with [X]% recycled polyester (by weight, excluding trims)" instead of "sustainable materials".
- **Qualified** — the claim plus the qualification that must travel with it: same place, same prominence, not a footnote three scrolls away. Check the channel has room for it; if packaging can't fit the qualification, the claim doesn't fit either.
- **Process, not outcome** — describe what the business does rather than an effect it can't prove. "We fund habitat restoration on [X] ha" rather than "we restore nature".
- **Remove it** — sometimes the right direction. Say so plainly when the underlying fact doesn't support any claim.

## Rules

- Never invent facts, figures, certifications, or methodologies. Use placeholders like `[X]%`, `[baseline year]`, `[certification scheme]` for anything the user has to supply.
- Don't swap one vague term for another ("eco" → "conscious", "sustainable" → "responsible"). That moves the problem, it doesn't fix it.
- Keep the brand's voice where you can, and write in the original language; offer a translation if useful.
- Never describe a direction as compliant, approved, or safe — "lower-risk" at most.

## Evidence by claim type

| Claim type | Evidence typically needed |
| --- | --- |
| Recycled or recycled-content | Percentage by weight, which components it covers, chain-of-custody certification for the recycled input (e.g. a third-party recycled-content standard), date of data. |
| Recyclable | Which components; whether collection and recycling actually exist at scale in the markets where it's sold; any conditions (remove the sleeve, rinse). |
| Emissions reduced | Footprint method (e.g. GHG Protocol, ISO 14067 for products), boundary and scopes, baseline year, the reduction achieved within the value chain, third-party verification. Offsets financed outside the value chain can be described separately but never netted into a product claim. |
| Future targets ("net zero by 2040") | A detailed, realistic implementation plan with measurable, time-bound interim targets; periodic verification by an independent expert; plan and findings publicly available. |
| Sustainability label or badge | A certification scheme meeting the EmpCo definition (third-party, public requirements, independent monitoring) or a label established by public authorities. Otherwise drop the badge. |
| Generic claims ("eco-friendly", "green") | Recognised excellent environmental performance — EU Ecolabel, an official ISO 14024 type I ecolabel, or top performance under Union law. Without it, replace with a specific claim. |
| Comparisons ("50% less water") | What it's compared to, the method, the date of the data, and how it's kept up to date. |
| Durability and repair | Tested lifetime in usage time or intensity, spare-part availability and duration, repair information, software support period. |
| Social claims (fair pay, working conditions, animal welfare) | Audit results or certification, their scope (which suppliers, which tiers), and their date. |
| Nature and biodiversity | See below. |

## Nature and biodiversity claims

Nature claims fail most often by claiming an outcome when the evidence covers an activity, or a whole product when the evidence covers one landscape. For each direction, pin down:

- **Activity or outcome.** "We fund restoration on 400 ha" (activity) is not "we increased biodiversity" (outcome). An outcome claim needs measured change against a baseline.
- **Extent or condition.** Hectares protected or restored measure extent. Biodiversity improvement is about condition — species, habitat quality, ecosystem function — and needs its own metrics.
- **Practice or status.** "Uses cover crops" is a detected practice; "regenerative" is a status with no single agreed definition. Name the practices, and the standard if one is used.
- **Measurement and attribution.** A measured change on land doesn't show that the business's programme caused it. Say what was measured, how, over what period, and whether causation is established or assumed.
- **Linkage.** Landscape results must be tied to the supplier, ingredient, programme, batch, or SKU being marketed. A regional project doesn't make an unlinked product "nature positive".
- **Permanence and monitoring.** How long the outcome is secured, who monitors it, and how often results are updated.
- **Credits.** Biodiversity or nature credits financed elsewhere follow the same logic as carbon offsets: the contribution can be described, but it can't be turned into a claim that the product is neutral or positive for nature.
- **"Nature positive."** No agreed definition exists for a product or company claim. Treat it as generic unless tied to a specific, measured, verified outcome — and usually recommend a specific direction instead.
